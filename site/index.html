Why it matters

A bad grid date costs money long before anyone notices

Land options, surveys and planning applications are paid for up front. If the site cannot connect for ten years, that money is tied up or lost, and someone signed off on it. The answer is in public records, but it is spread across registers that few people have time to read. A short, sourced report means the decision rests on evidence, not on a guess.

Gates explained

Gate 1, Gate 2 and blank: what the labels in the register mean

Since connections reform, NESO sorts the queue by whether a project is ready and needed. The label tells you how firm a project's date and place in the queue are. Think of it as the difference between a reserved seat and a standby ticket.

Gate 2

firm

A firm offer, for projects that showed NESO they are ready (for example, land secured) and needed for the 2030 clean power plan.

  • Confirmed connection date
  • Confirmed connection point
  • Secure place in the reordered queue

Like a reserved seat. You know where you sit and when the show starts.

136 unbuilt projects, 25.4 GW. Average wait 1.5 years.

Gate 1

indicative

A holding position for projects not yet ready or needed. They can apply for Gate 2 later.

  • Indicative date only, which can move
  • Indicative location only
  • No firm place in the queue

Like a standby ticket. You are in the building, but your seat and time can change.

926 unbuilt projects, 333.0 GW. Average wait 8.1 years.

Blank

not stated

The register's Gate field is empty. The register does not say why, so we do not treat these as either gate.

  • Status not stated in the register
  • Ask the project or NESO before relying on it

No ticket shown. The register does not say which kind it holds.

615 unbuilt projects, 233.6 GW.

Connections reform
31 Oct

On 25 September 2026 NESO asked Ofgem to move the Gate 2 Phase 1 offer deadline from 30 September to 31 October 2026. A further extension has been reported but we have not found it confirmed.

So what: firm offers are arriving later than planned, so dates built on them may slip.

In plain English

New wind farms, solar parks and batteries cannot plug into the grid whenever they like. They join a queue, and right now about 592 GW of projects are in it.

The queue is public, but it lives in large spreadsheets and documents that change every week. We read them, work out what changed, and tell you what it means for the site you care about, with the sources attached so you can check.
